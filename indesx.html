Educator philosophy

Let them struggle a little. Then let them figure it out.

Inquiry-basedCulturally responsiveNeuro-inclusiveBilingual-ready

I believe kids learn science by doing science. If I lecture, they remember my words for a day. If they wrestle with a puzzle — why the paper-cup phone goes quiet, why one bridge holds and another sags — and then crack it themselves, they keep it. I call that productive struggle, and my job is to keep it productive: the right question at the right moment, then enough wait time to let them think.

Northern New Mexico’s learners come from rural towns, Pueblo communities, Hispanic families with deep roots, and every kind of brain. I plan for all of them from the start, with local examples, Spanish vocabulary support, and more than one way into every activity.

02 · K–12 Curriculum

One big idea, three grade levels

Every lesson starts at the end. Using Backward Design, I first decide what students must be able to explain, then how I’ll know they can, and only then pick the activity. The 5E model (Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate) gives each lesson the same five-step shape, so students always explore before I explain anything. Here, one theme, waves that carry information, grows from a 2nd grader’s string telephone to an 11th grader’s claim about how satellites send data.

Each lesson is substitute-ready: supply list, minute-by-minute pacing, safety notes, and differentiated handouts for struggling learners and English language learners.

Zap a rock. Read its rainbow.

On Mars, a rover shoots a laser at a rock. The rock flashes with a tiny spark of light. Scientists split that light into colors, like a rainbow. Each element makes its own pattern of colors.
